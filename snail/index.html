The Snake genre is one of the oldest in gaming. It began with Blockade, a 1976 arcade game by Gremlin. Two lines, each leaving a wall behind it, and whoever crashed first lost. I first encountered it on the Atari 2600 as Surround, one of the launch cartridges for that console. Twenty years later the same idea became Snake on Nokia phones, and became synonymous with the genre.

Blockade's designer, Lane Hauck, got the idea from a physics puzzle called the drunk and the lamppost. Picture someone standing at a lamppost who takes a step in a random direction, then another, then another, never planning where to go. That is a random walk: every step is a coin flip, and the path just wanders. Hauck wrote a program that moved an arrow up, down, left, or right at random, then added one rule: it could not step onto a square it had already visited. Put two of those walkers in the hands of players, and you have Blockade. Random walks show up all over science, and I'm building a page where you can watch them for yourself. Check back soon.

This is an attempt to reimagine the idea for the current moment: remove the 90 degree angles and other limitations of the original hardware, and add touch interaction. I also wanted to give it a little more grounding in gaming culture.

In 2014, on the Rooster Teeth podcast, Gavin Free asked whether you would take $10 million and immortality if an immortal snail followed you forever and its touch killed you. It became a meme, the immortal snail, also called the snail assassin. So money piles up while you run, everything you pick up makes you slower, and the snail never stops. Good luck!
